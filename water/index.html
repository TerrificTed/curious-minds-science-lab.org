MODULE 1 · CASE STUDY: FRESH WATER IN SINGAPORE 55 MIN
1 · A 🇸🇬 The Island With No Big Rivers — Tap 1: Imported Water 10 min ▼
How does scientific understanding help a country with almost no rivers supply clean water to everyone?

Imagine a country so small you could drive right across it in under an hour. That is Singapore: an island of about 735 square kilometres at the tip of the Malay Peninsula, home to more than 6 million people. It has no big natural lakes, no long rivers and no huge underground stores of water. And yet when anyone in Singapore turns on a tap, clean water you can drink comes out — every time, for everyone.

The strange thing is that Singapore is not dry at all. It gets about 2,500 mm of rain a year — nearly four times as much as London. The problem is storage: on a small, crowded island there is very little room to keep the rain before it runs off into the sea. In 1963–64 a long dry spell meant water had to be rationed — taps in many homes were turned off for part of the day for months. Singapore's leaders decided that must never happen again, and they turned to scientists and engineers for the answer.

The answer is called the Four National Taps: four completely different ways of getting water, run together as one system, so that if one tap struggles the others can make up the difference. Today Singapore uses about 440 million gallons a day — around 2 billion litres, enough to fill roughly 800 Olympic swimming pools every single day — and that could almost double by 2065. Here are the four taps: (1) imported water, (2) local catchments (rain collected on the island), (3) NEWater (used water made ultra-clean) and (4) desalinated water (seawater made fresh). We start with the oldest.

Tap 1 — Imported water. Just across a narrow strait to the north is the Malaysian state of Johor, which has a big river, the Johor River. Huge pipelines carry water from Johor alongside the Causeway — the road-and-rail bridge that joins the two countries. Under an agreement signed in 1962, Singapore may draw up to 250 million gallons a day from the Johor River until 2061. Singapore's water agency runs the waterworks in Johor that cleans the water, and it sends some treated water back to Johor too. Sharing a river between two countries takes trust, rules and good science — and it is exactly why Singapore built three more taps: a smart system never depends on just one source.

1 · B 🌧️ Tap 2: Local Catchments — Catching Every Raindrop 25 min ▼

This is the tap that makes Singapore truly unusual. A catchment is any area of land where the rain that falls is collected and flows to the same place. In most countries, catchments are forests and hills far from the city. In Singapore, the city itself is the catchment. About two-thirds of the island's land is water catchment, and rain that falls on roofs, roads, playgrounds and car parks is channelled through drains, canals and rivers into 17 reservoirs. Think of the whole country as a giant funnel.

There are two kinds of catchment. Protected catchments are nature reserves in the middle of the island, where forest is kept on purpose so the rain running off it stays clean. MacRitchie Reservoir, built in 1868, is the oldest — you can still walk the forest trails around it. Urban catchments are everywhere else: the busy city, where the rain must be collected from streets and buildings through thousands of kilometres of drains. Rivers were dammed at their mouths to turn them into reservoirs, and in 2008 the biggest of these projects opened: the Marina Barrage, a dam across the mouth of the Marina Channel right in the city centre. Behind it, the salty sea water was slowly flushed out and replaced by rain until the bay became a freshwater lake — Singapore's 15th reservoir, collecting rain from a catchment of 10,000 hectares (about 14,000 football pitches of city). The barrage does a second job too: in a big storm at high tide, its gates and giant pumps push excess water out to sea so the low-lying city centre does not flood.

Here is the problem with collecting rain from a city: whatever the rain washes off the ground ends up in the reservoir. Litter, oil from roads — and, biggest of all, soil. When land is left bare, for example on a building site, raindrops smash into the loose earth and wash it away. This is soil erosion, and the muddy water it makes is full of silt (very fine soil particles). Silt is bad news: it clogs drains so they overflow and flood; it makes reservoir water cloudy and harder and more expensive to clean; it slowly fills up reservoirs so they hold less water; and it smothers the fish and plants that live in them. So every building site in Singapore must use Earth Control Measures: covering bare soil with sheets or grass, putting up silt fences, building settling ponds so mud sinks before water leaves the site, and washing lorry wheels before they drive out. The water agency even uses cameras that watch drains for muddy water and alert inspectors.

Since 2006, Singapore has gone one step further with the ABC Waters programme — Active, Beautiful, Clean. Instead of hiding rainwater in straight concrete channels, engineers turn some of them back into natural-looking rivers lined with plants. At Bishan–Ang Mo Kio Park, a straight concrete canal was dug up and replaced by a winding river with grassy banks, finished in 2012. The plants slow the water down, their roots hold the soil in place, and their leaves and stems filter out dirt before it reaches the reservoir. Otters moved back in. Rainwater, it turns out, is easier to keep clean when people can see it, enjoy it and care about it — that is responsibility built into the design of a city.

HANDS-ON · THE BOTTLE EROSION TEST (8 min, or set up today and test tomorrow)

You need: three empty plastic drinks bottles (same size), scissors (grown-up job), soil from the garden, a handful of leaves, twigs or bark, a small patch of grass or moss with its roots (or seeds sown a week earlier), three clear cups and a jug of water.
  1. Lay each bottle on its side and cut a big window out of the top side, so each one becomes a long tray. Keep the lids off — the neck is the spout.
  2. Fill bottle 1 with bare soil. Fill bottle 2 with soil covered in leaves, twigs and bark. Fill bottle 3 with soil plus the grass or moss, roots and all.
  3. Line them up on a table edge with the necks hanging over and a cup under each neck. Tilt them slightly downhill.
  4. Pour the same amount of water, slowly, onto the far end of each tray — a raincloud in a jug. Then compare the three cups.
What is going on: raindrops hitting bare soil knock loose particles free, and running water carries them away — erosion, in miniature. Leaves and bark act like an umbrella that softens each drop. Roots act like a net, gripping the soil so it cannot move. That is exactly why Singapore covers building sites and plants its riverbanks: the clearer the water that reaches the reservoir, the less work it takes to make it drinkable.

1 · C ♻️ Tap 3: NEWater — Used Water Made Ultra-Clean 10 min ▼

Every time you shower, wash your hands, brush your teeth or flush the toilet, the water goes down the plughole and away. In most places it is cleaned just enough to pour into the sea. Singapore asked a bold question: what if we cleaned it so well that we could use it again? The answer is NEWater, first supplied in 2003.

Used water from homes travels through sewers into the Deep Tunnel Sewerage System — a 206 km network of giant tunnels deep underground that carries it, mostly by gravity, to huge water reclamation plants. There it gets a normal clean-up: screens catch rubbish, grit sinks, and helpful bacteria eat most of the dissolved waste. Then the cleaned-up water goes to a NEWater factory for three extra steps.
Step 1 — Microfiltration. Water is pushed through membranes full of tiny holes, about a five-hundredth of the width of a hair. Bacteria, tiny particles and anything bigger are stopped.
Step 2 — Reverse osmosis. The water is squeezed under pressure through a membrane so fine that almost only water molecules get through. Viruses, salts, and leftover chemicals such as traces of medicines are left behind.
Step 3 — Ultraviolet (UV) light. As a final safety check, the water flows past strong UV lamps. UV light scrambles the DNA of any microbe that might somehow have slipped through, so it cannot make anyone ill.

The result is water so pure it is checked against around 300 different tests and beats the World Health Organization's standards for drinking water. Most NEWater goes to factories — especially those making computer chips, which need water purer than tap water — and to cooling systems in big buildings. In dry spells, some is added to reservoirs, where it mixes with rainwater and is treated again before reaching taps. NEWater can already meet up to 40% of Singapore's water needs, and that is planned to rise to more than half by 2060. Water that used to be thrown away is now one of the country's biggest sources.

1 · D 🌊 Tap 4: Desalination — Turning the Sea Into Drinking Water 10 min ▼

Singapore is surrounded by water — it just happens to be salty. Every litre of seawater has about 35 grams of salt dissolved in it, roughly seven teaspoons. Drink it and your body has to use even more water to get rid of the salt, so you end up thirstier than before. Desalination means taking the salt out.

There are two main ways to do it. The old way is to boil seawater and collect the steam, which is pure water — the salt stays behind. It works, but boiling huge amounts of water takes enormous amounts of energy. The modern way, used in Singapore, is reverse osmosis — the same idea as the second step in NEWater, but harder. Seawater is pumped at very high pressure, more than twenty-five times the pressure in a car tyre, against special membranes. Water molecules are forced through; salt is left behind as extra-salty water called brine, which is carefully mixed back into the sea.

Singapore opened its first desalination plant in 2005 and now has five. The newest are clever: the Keppel Marina East plant (2020) can treat seawater in dry weather or rainwater from Marina Reservoir in wet weather, and it hides its machinery underground beneath a grassy roof where people can walk. Desalination has one huge advantage — the sea never runs dry, even in a drought — and one big drawback: it needs a lot of electricity, about 3.5 kilowatt-hours for every 1,000 litres. That makes it the most expensive of the four taps, so scientists in Singapore are working hard to find membranes and methods that use less energy. That is why desalination is the back-up that never fails, rather than the only tap.

HANDS-ON · BUILD A SOLAR DESALINATOR (set up 5 min, then leave in the sun for a few hours)

You need: a large bowl, a short heavy cup or glass that fits inside it, warm water, two teaspoons of salt, cling film, a small stone or coin, and a sunny windowsill or garden spot.
  1. Stir the salt into about 3 cm of warm water in the big bowl. Taste a tiny drop on your finger — that is your "seawater".
  2. Stand the empty cup in the middle of the bowl. The salty water must not reach the top of the cup.
  3. Stretch cling film tightly over the bowl. Put the stone on the film right above the cup so the film dips in the middle.
  4. Leave it in strong sunshine for a few hours. Then carefully lift the film and taste the water that has collected in the cup.
What is going on: the Sun warms the water, and some of it evaporates — only the water molecules float off as vapour; the salt is far too heavy to come with them. The vapour hits the cooler cling film, condenses into drops, runs down to the lowest point and drips into the cup. You have built a tiny version of the old "boil and collect" desalination, powered by sunshine — and a tiny version of the water cycle you will meet in Module 2.

◆ MODULE 1 IN ONE BREATH

Singapore has lots of rain but almost nowhere to keep it, so it built four taps that work as one system: water shared with a neighbour, rain caught from a whole city (and kept clean by stopping soil erosion), used water purified with membranes and UV light, and seawater made fresh. No single tap could do the job. Together, guided by science, they keep water flowing for over 6 million people.
Some of the clever thinking in membranes is borrowed from nature. Every cell in your body has special channels called aquaporins that let water molecules through one at a time and block almost everything else. Scientists — including a team in Singapore — have been building membranes with aquaporins inside them to make water purification use less energy.
MODULE 2 · EARTH'S SYSTEMS AND HOW WATER FLOWS 25 MIN
2 🌍 Earth's Four Systems — and the Water That Joins Them 25 min ▼
How many of Earth's major systems exist, and how do natural processes like erosion shape them?

Singapore's water works because it is a system — lots of parts connected so that a change in one affects the others. Earth itself works the same way. Scientists divide our planet into four major systems, sometimes called the four "spheres":
🌊 The hydrosphere — all of Earth's water: oceans, rivers, lakes, rain, water underground, and the frozen water in glaciers and ice caps.
⛰️ The geosphere — the solid Earth: rocks, mountains, sand, soil and the hot layers deep inside.
☁️ The atmosphere — the blanket of air around the planet, including the water vapour that makes clouds.
🌿 The biosphere — every living thing: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria — and you.

These four are not separate boxes. They are constantly swapping things with each other, and water is the great connector. The Sun warms the ocean and water evaporates into the atmosphere (hydrosphere → atmosphere). It cools, condenses into clouds and falls as rain (atmosphere → hydrosphere and geosphere). Some soaks into the soil and rock as groundwater; some runs off into rivers. Plants drink it through their roots and breathe it out through their leaves — transpiration (geosphere → biosphere → atmosphere). This never-ending loop is the water cycle, and it has been running for around four billion years. There is about the same amount of water on Earth now as when the dinosaurs lived, which means some of the water in your glass has probably passed through a dinosaur.

As water travels, it reshapes the land. First comes weathering: rain, frost, plant roots and even chemicals in water slowly crack and crumble rock into smaller pieces. Then comes erosion: moving water, wind and ice carry those pieces away. Finally there is deposition: when the water slows down, it drops what it was carrying — which is how beaches, river deltas and new land form. Give erosion enough time and it can carve the Grand Canyon. Give it a rainstorm and a bare building site, and it fills a drain with mud, as you saw in Singapore.

The most precious thing erosion moves is soil. Soil is not just dirt: it is crumbled rock mixed with rotting leaves, air, water, fungi and billions of tiny living things — a meeting point of all four spheres. Healthy soil acts like a sponge and a filter, soaking up rain, slowly releasing it into rivers and cleaning it on the way to underground water. It forms incredibly slowly: according to the UN Food and Agriculture Organization, making just 1 cm of soil can take up to 1,000 years — but a single storm can wash it away from bare ground in an afternoon. When people cut down forests or leave land bare, soil erodes, rivers turn brown, reservoirs fill with silt, and the land holds less water. Protecting soil is protecting water.

MODULE 3 · FINITE VS INFINITE RESOURCES & GLOBAL DEMAND 25 MIN
3 ⚖️ Finite or Infinite? Water and a Thirsty World 25 min ▼
What are finite and infinite resources, and how does global demand impact them?

A resource is anything from nature that people use. Scientists sort resources into two big groups. Infinite (or renewable) resources will not run out however much we use them, because nature keeps supplying them: sunlight, wind and the tides will keep coming for billions of years. Finite (or non-renewable) resources exist in a fixed amount, or form so slowly that once we use them up they are effectively gone: coal, oil and gas took millions of years to form; metals like gold and copper are only in the ground once.

Water is a puzzle. On one hand, the total amount of water on Earth hardly changes, and the water cycle keeps cleaning and returning it as rain — so water is renewable. On the other hand, the useful part is tiny, and if we take fresh water from a river, lake or underground store faster than rain can refill it, it behaves exactly like a finite resource. Some deep underground water stores, called aquifers, filled up over thousands of years; pump them out in a few decades and they will not be back in your lifetime. So the best way to describe fresh water is: renewable, but limited — it is only renewable if we use it wisely.

Interactive thinking task. Here is how Earth's water is shared out. Out of all of it, about 97% is salty ocean water. About 2% is frozen in glaciers and ice caps. Only about 1% is fresh water that people, farms and wildlife can actually reach — and most of that 1% is hidden underground. Every person, every farm, every factory, every river creature on the planet shares that one small slice. Try the 100 Cups activity below before you read on.

Why demand matters. In 1950 there were about 2.5 billion people on Earth. Today there are over 8 billion, and every one of them needs water to drink, cook, wash and — above all — to grow food: farming uses about 70% of all the fresh water people take from rivers, lakes and the ground. Factories need water to make almost everything, from cars to computer chips to clothes. As the population grows and countries build more industry, the demand for that same 1% climbs and climbs. Already, according to the World Health Organization and UNICEF, about 1 in 4 people in the world do not have safe drinking water at home.

There is a brand-new kind of demand, too. Artificial intelligence (AI) runs on enormous buildings full of computers called data centres, and those computer chips get very hot. Many data centres cool them with water that evaporates in cooling towers — about 80% of the water they take is lost to the air. A single large data centre can use as much water in a day as a town of 10,000 to 50,000 people, and companies around the world are planning to build many more of them for AI. Engineers are working on answers — sealed "closed-loop" cooling that reuses the same water, cooling with recycled water instead of drinking water, or building in cooler places — but it is a perfect example of causation: a new technology causes a new strain on an old resource.

HANDS-ON · ALL THE WATER IN THE WORLD IN A JUG (5 min)

You need: a 1-litre jug of water, a measuring cup or kitchen scales, a small glass, a teaspoon, an ice-cube tray, salt, and a dropper or straw.
  1. Your 1 litre of water is all the water on Earth. Pour out 970 ml into a bowl and stir in a spoon of salt: that is the ocean.
  2. Pour 20 ml into the ice-cube tray and put it in the freezer: that is the water locked in glaciers and ice caps.
  3. The 10 ml left (two teaspoons) is all the fresh water people can reach. Most of it is underground.
  4. Now use a dropper or straw to take out a single drop. That drop is roughly all the water in the world's lakes and rivers.
What is going on: the jug makes the 97–2–1 split something you can see and hold. Look at the bowl of seawater next to your two teaspoons. Now imagine 8 billion people, every farm and every factory sharing those two teaspoons — and remember that the water cycle refills them only as fast as it rains. That is why using fresh water wisely matters so much.

MODULE 4 · SCIENCE THAT PROTECTS FRESH WATER — AND YOUR PART 15 MIN
4 🔬 Water Inventors, Water Detectives — and You 15 min ▼
How are scientists protecting fresh water around the world, and how can we contribute?

You have already seen how Singapore recycles and desalinates water. Around the world, scientists are attacking the problem from completely different directions. Here are six of the most exciting.

☁️ Catching fog. In some of the driest places on Earth — Chile's Atacama Desert and the mountains of southern Morocco — it almost never rains, but thick fog rolls in from the sea. Giant mesh fog nets catch the tiny droplets, which join together, trickle down and are collected in pipes. In Morocco, fog nets supply drinking water to whole villages.
🏜️ Pulling water out of desert air. Chemist Omar Yaghi designs crystals called metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) that are full of tiny holes, like a sponge built atom by atom. At night they soak up water vapour from even very dry air; when the Sun warms them, they release it as liquid water. His team has harvested drinking water from the desert air of Arizona, and in 2025 he shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for inventing MOFs.
🛰️ Weighing water from space. NASA's GRACE satellites (launched 2002) and their successors GRACE-FO (2018) measure tiny changes in Earth's gravity as they fly over it. Water is heavy, so when an underground aquifer is pumped out, the pull of gravity there gets very slightly weaker — and the satellites notice. For the first time, scientists can see where hidden groundwater is shrinking.
💧 Farming drop by drop. Since farms use about 70% of fresh water, saving water on farms matters most. Drip irrigation, developed in Israel in the 1960s, uses thin pipes with tiny holes to drip water straight onto each plant's roots instead of spraying whole fields, so far less is lost to evaporation and run-off. Soil sensors now tell farmers exactly when plants are thirsty.
🧽 Sponge cities. Since 2015, China has been rebuilding parts of its cities to act like sponges: roads and pavements that let water soak through, rooftop gardens, rain gardens and new wetlands that soak up storm water, clean it and store it underground instead of letting it rush away into drains. (Sound familiar? It is the same idea as Singapore's ABC Waters, scaled up.)
🦫 Nature's engineers. In the UK and elsewhere, scientists have brought back beavers. Beaver dams slow rivers down, store water in ponds through dry summers, trap silt, and reduce flooding downstream. Sometimes the best technology is an animal that has been doing the job for millions of years.

And you do not have to be a grown-up. When she was 11, Gitanjali Rao heard about a city in the USA whose tap water had become contaminated with lead. She invented Tethys, a small device that uses tiny carbon tubes and a phone app to test water for lead quickly and cheaply — and won America's Top Young Scientist award. In 2020 TIME magazine named her its first-ever Kid of the Year. Scientific understanding really does inform the preservation of Earth's resources — and it can start at your kitchen table.

HANDS-ON · BE A WATER DETECTIVE: THE SILENT LEAK TEST (5 min + 15 min waiting)

You need: a grown-up, a toilet with a tank (cistern) you can lift the lid off, and a few drops of food colouring.
  1. Ask a grown-up to lift the lid off the toilet tank. Add 5–10 drops of food colouring to the water in the tank (not the bowl).
  2. Do not flush for 15 minutes. Go and do something else.
  3. Come back and look in the toilet bowl. If coloured water has appeared there, water is leaking from the tank all day long without anyone noticing. Then flush to clear the colour.
  4. Bonus mission: with your grown-up, check every tap in the house for drips. Put a cup under any dripping tap for 10 minutes, measure what you collect, and multiply by 144 to find how much it wastes in a day.
What is going on: a worn rubber seal at the bottom of the tank can let water trickle into the bowl silently, day and night. A badly leaking toilet can waste hundreds of litres a day — more in one day than a whole family drinks in a month. Finding it is real detective work: a hypothesis, a test, an observation and a conclusion. If you find a leak, you have just done the most useful water-saving job in your house.

◆ THE THREE THINGS TO REMEMBER

1. Fresh water is renewable, but limited. Only about 1 cup in every 100 of Earth's water is fresh water we can reach, and the water cycle refills it only as fast as it rains. Use it faster than that and it runs out.
2. Water connects everything. It flows through all four Earth systems, shapes the land through erosion, and carries soil with it — so protecting soil, forests and rivers is protecting water.
3. Science plus responsibility works. Singapore turned rain, used water and seawater into a reliable supply for over 6 million people. Scientists are catching fog, pulling water out of desert air and weighing it from space. And every tap you turn off is part of the same story.
Gitanjali Rao was 11 when she started building her lead-detecting device — about your age. Her advice to other kids: find a problem you care about, research it, and don't be afraid to fail on the way. What water problem would you invent a solution for?

◷ LATEST NUMBERS — CHECKED NOVEMBER 2026

Water systems change, so the numbers most likely to go out of date live in this box.

Singapore's water use: about 440 million gallons (around 2 billion litres) a day, roughly 60% by industry and businesses and 40% by homes; expected to almost double by 2065 (PUB).
NEWater: can meet up to 40% of current demand, planned to rise to more than half by 2060. The first NEWater factory, at Bedok, closed in 2024 after reaching the end of its life; new factories at Changi and Tuas are being added.
Desalination: five plants (SingSpring 2005, Tuas South 2013, Tuas 2018, Keppel Marina East 2020, Jurong Island 2022), using about 3.5 kWh of energy per 1,000 litres.
Household use: about 141 litres per person per day in Singapore homes in 2023; the national target is 130 litres by 2030.
Safe drinking water: about 1 in 4 people worldwide (around 2.1 billion) still lack safely managed drinking water at home (WHO/UNICEF, August 2025).
AI data centres: a large data centre can use up to about 19 million litres (5 million US gallons) of water a day for cooling (EESI, 2025). Estimates for AI are changing fast as new centres are built and new cooling methods arrive.
