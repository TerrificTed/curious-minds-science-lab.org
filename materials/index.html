PART 01 🧱 What Are Materials? 10 min ▼

Look at anything near you right now — this screen, the table, your own hand. Every single one of them is made of atoms: particles so small that a single full stop on a page holds more of them than there are people on Earth, many times over. There are only about 90 kinds of atom found in nature (hydrogen, carbon, iron, gold, oxygen and so on — the elements), and every material in the universe is built from that short list, the way every LEGO model is built from the same handful of brick shapes.

Here is the twist that makes materials science a real subject. It is not only which atoms you use — it is how they are arranged. Take pure carbon atoms and stack them in flat sheets that slide over one another, and you get graphite, the soft grey stuff in a pencil. Take the exact same carbon atoms and lock each one to four neighbours in a rigid 3-D lattice, and you get diamond, the hardest natural material known. Same ingredients, different recipe, completely different result. Scientists sort materials into four big families — metals (atoms in a neat crystal with loose electrons, so they conduct and bend), ceramics like glass and pottery (atoms locked tight, so they are hard but snap), polymers like plastic and rubber (long spaghetti-chains of atoms, so they are light and bendy), and composites (two materials teamed up, like carbon fibre in a bike frame).

Try this in the kitchen before you go on. Put a spoon of coarse sugar in one glass of water and a spoon of fine caster sugar in another, stir both the same, and watch. The fine sugar vanishes first. But it is exactly the same material — same atoms, same arrangement. So why does it behave differently? Because there is more of it touching the water. Cut a lump into smaller pieces and you create new faces, and new faces mean more surface area. Keep that idea in your pocket — it is the single most important idea in the whole of nanotechnology, and you will meet it again in Part 03.

People say "solid things are, well, solid." They are not. An atom is a tiny, heavy nucleus surrounded by a cloud of electrons — and if the nucleus were the size of a marble sitting in the middle of a football stadium, the electrons would be buzzing around the outer seats. Everything in between is empty. A table feels solid only because the electron clouds of its atoms push back against the electron clouds of your hand. You have never actually touched anything in your life — not in the sense of two atoms bumping.
A glass of water holds roughly 10,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 water molecules — ten trillion trillion. That is so many that if you tipped the glass into the sea, waited for it to spread evenly through every ocean on Earth, and then scooped a fresh glass from anywhere, you would still get about a thousand of your original molecules back.
PART 02 🔧 Material Properties: Why Engineers Choose 10 min ▼

Pick up a paperclip and bend it. It bends — and it stays bent. Now stretch a rubber band and let go. It snaps straight back. Both are "bendy", but in completely different ways, and materials scientists have a word for each. The paperclip is ductile: metal atoms sit in neat rows that can slide past each other and then settle into a new position. The rubber band is elastic: its long tangled polymer chains straighten out when you pull and coil back up when you let go. Bend the paperclip back and forth twenty times and it snaps — that is fatigue, and it is why aeroplane wings are checked so carefully.

Every material has a whole report card of properties like this. Strength — how much pulling or squashing it takes before it breaks. Hardness — how well it resists being scratched (diamond wins; talc loses). Toughness — whether it bends before breaking or shatters like glass. Density — how heavy a lump of it is. Conductivity — whether electricity and heat can flow through it (copper: yes, very; plastic: almost not at all). Transparency, melting point, and whether it rusts, rots or stays shiny for a thousand years.

This is where engineering starts: every design is a choice of material, and there is never a perfect one — only the best trade-off for the job. A bridge cable must be strong and tough (steel). A phone case must be light and not conduct electricity (polymer). A frying pan must conduct heat brilliantly (metal) but its handle must not (plastic or wood). A bulletproof vest must stop a bullet but still let you move (Kevlar, a polymer whose chains line up so neatly it is five times stronger than steel for its weight). Choose wrong and things break, melt, rust or electrocute someone. Choose cleverly and you get a bicycle that weighs less than a cat.

People say "strong" and "hard" mean the same thing. They do not — and the difference matters. Diamond is the hardest natural material (nothing can scratch it) but hit it with a hammer and it shatters, because it is not tough. Spider silk is soft enough to squash between your fingers, yet for its weight it is stronger than steel, and it can stretch by nearly a third before it breaks. Hard means "resists scratching". Strong means "resists breaking under a pull". Tough means "bends and absorbs a whack instead of cracking". A good engineer needs all three words.
Pure water is actually a poor conductor of electricity. What makes tap water, sea water and sweat conduct is the salt dissolved in them — the salt splits into charged particles called ions, and the ions carry the current. That is why you should never handle electrical things with wet hands: your wet skin has become a salty wire.
PART 03 🔬 What Is Nano? 10 min ▼

A nanometre (nm) is one-billionth of a metre. Write it out: 0.000000001 m. That is so small it is hard to picture, so here is a ladder. A metre stick is 1,000,000,000 nm long. Your hand — about 15 cm — is around 150,000,000 nm. A single human hair is 80,000–100,000 nm wide. A red blood cell is about 7,000 nm. A bacterium, 1,000–2,000 nm. A virus, around 100 nm. A strand of DNA is just 2 nm across. And a single atom is about a tenth of a nanometre. Nanotechnology is the science of building and using things in the range of roughly 1 to 100 nm — bigger than an atom, smaller than a virus, and far, far too small to see with any ordinary microscope. Here is a fact to try on your family tonight: your fingernails grow at about 1 nanometre every second.

Why does anyone care about a size? Because at the nanoscale, materials stop behaving the way they do in your hand. Two reasons. First, the sugar-cube idea from Part 01 goes into overdrive: chop a lump into nano-sized pieces and almost every atom ends up on a surface, with nothing above it. Surfaces are where all the action is — where things dissolve, react, stick and catch light — so nano-sized materials react faster, melt at lower temperatures and grab hold of things more fiercely than big lumps of the same stuff. Second, at a few nanometres the strange rules of quantum physics take over. Gold that is nano-sized is not gold-coloured at all: a jar of gold nanoparticles in water glows ruby red or purple, and the exact colour depends only on how big the particles are. Medieval glassmakers were using this without knowing it — the red in old cathedral windows is often gold nanoparticles.

Kurzgesagt has a brilliant way into this idea. An ant can fall from a skyscraper and walk away; a mouse would probably survive; an elephant… would not. It is not about being brave. It is about surface area versus volume: as something gets smaller, its surface (which catches the air) shrinks far more slowly than its weight. Shrink all the way down to nano and the surface completely takes over — which is exactly why nano is different.

Nanometre One-Billionth 1–100 nm Surface Area vs Volume Quantum Effects Gold Nanoparticles Scale
People say "nano just means really small." Not quite. Lots of things are really small — a grain of flour, a speck of dust, a red blood cell — and none of them are nano. Nano is a specific band, about 1 to 100 nanometres, and it earns its own name because that is the size where materials change their behaviour: colour, melting point, strength, how they react. A speck of dust is just a small rock. A nanoparticle of the same rock is a new material.
The Romans made a cup in the 4th century — the Lycurgus Cup, now in the British Museum — that looks jade green in ordinary light but glows blood red when you shine a light through it. In 1990 scientists worked out why: the glass contains tiny particles of gold and silver about 50–100 nm across. The Romans had made a nanomaterial 1,600 years before anyone had a word for it.

Pick any two (each takes 5–8 minutes), and for each one make your prediction first — press the button under the experiment before you touch anything. Scientists always guess first, then test. ACTIVITY A · MAKE GRAPHENE WITH A PENCIL (8 min)

You need: a soft pencil (6B, 4B or 2B is best; an ordinary HB works but is fainter), a sheet of paper, a small battery (a 3 V coin cell is perfect), an LED, and two short wires or strips of kitchen foil.
  1. Scribble hard on the paper until you have a solid, shiny dark patch about the size of a matchbox. Go over it at least ten times — thickness matters.
  2. Connect the battery's + side to the LED's long leg with one wire. Touch the LED's short leg to one end of your patch, and the battery's − side to the other end.
  3. Watch the LED. Then slide the two contact points closer together, and further apart. Draw a long thin line instead of a patch and try again.
What is going on: pencil "lead" is not lead — it is graphite, a stack of carbon sheets each just one atom thick. A single one of those sheets is graphene: the thinnest material ever made, about 200 times stronger than steel for its weight, and a better conductor of electricity than copper. Every time you write with a pencil you are shearing off stacks of graphene layers and leaving them on the paper — and because they conduct, your scribble is a real wire. In 2004 two scientists in Manchester peeled single graphene layers off a lump of graphite using nothing but sticky tape, and won the Nobel Prize for it in 2010. The longer and thinner your line, the harder the electrons find it to get through, so the LED dims — you have built a dimmer switch out of carbon.
Prediction: when you connect the LED through the graphite patch, will it light?

ACTIVITY B · LIQUID CRYSTALS THAT CHANGE COLOUR (5 min)

You need: a forehead strip thermometer, a mood ring, a colour-changing "thermochromic" sticker, mug or spoon — any one of these — plus a warm hand and something cold from the fridge.
  1. Look at its colour at room temperature. Now hold it firmly in your fist for 20 seconds and watch it change.
  2. Press it against the cold thing from the fridge. Which way does the colour go now?
  3. Breathe warm air on it. Try a friend's hand. Does everyone make the same colour?
What is going on: inside is a liquid crystal — a material that flows like a liquid but whose rod-shaped molecules line up in neat order like a crystal. In the kind used here the molecules stack in a gentle spiral, and the spiral's spacing is a few hundred nanometres — the same size as a wavelength of light. So the spiral reflects one exact colour. Warm it up and the molecules jostle, the spiral tightens, and the colour it reflects shifts (usually towards blue); cool it and it shifts back (towards red). That is a nano-sized structure changing shape, and you are watching it with your bare eyes. The same trick, controlled by electricity instead of heat, is how every LCD screen — laptops, calculators, older TVs — draws its picture.
Prediction: when you warm it in your fist, what happens?

ACTIVITY C · THE WATER THAT WON'T STICK (5 min)

You need: a dropper or teaspoon of water, an old cotton T-shirt, a rain jacket or umbrella, and — if you can find one — a cabbage leaf, nasturtium leaf or lotus leaf from the garden.
  1. Drip one drop of water onto the cotton. Watch what it does over ten seconds.
  2. Drip one drop onto the rain jacket. Tilt the fabric gently. Does the drop roll, or spread?
  3. Try the leaf. Sprinkle a little dust or cocoa powder on it first, then drip water on it and tilt.
What is going on: the cotton is hydrophilic — "water-loving" — its fibres pull the water in and it soaks. The jacket is hydrophobic — "water-fearing" — it has been given a coating a few nanometres thick that water molecules cannot grip, so the drop stays a ball and rolls away. The leaf is the champion, and it is the reason this science exists. A lotus leaf is covered in tiny bumps, and each bump is coated in even tinier crystals of wax, only nanometres across. The water drop can only touch the tips of the bumps, so it sits on a cushion of air, rolls off at the slightest tilt — and picks up the dirt as it goes. This is the lotus effect, and engineers copied it to make self-cleaning windows, paint and fabric. The bigger the drop's bounce, the more nano the surface.

People say pencils contain lead, and lead is poisonous, so don't chew your pencil. Half right. Pencils have never contained lead — when a huge deposit of graphite was found in England in the 1500s, people thought it was a kind of lead ore and the name stuck. The "lead" is graphite mixed with clay: more clay makes a harder, paler pencil (H), more graphite makes a softer, darker one (B). Graphite is harmless carbon. The don't-chew-it rule is still good advice, though — for the paint.
Liquid crystals were discovered in 1888 by an Austrian botanist, Friedrich Reinitzer, who was studying a substance from carrots and noticed it had two melting points — first into a cloudy liquid, then into a clear one. For nearly a century they were a curiosity with no use at all. Today there is a liquid-crystal display in almost every calculator, watch, petrol pump and microwave on Earth.
PART 05 🌍 Nanotechnology in Real Life 10 min ▼

You met nanotechnology before breakfast today, and you will meet it a dozen more times before bed. Sunscreen: the old kind was a thick white paste of zinc oxide — lifeguards' noses. Grind the same zinc oxide into nanoparticles and it turns transparent on your skin while still bouncing away the sun's ultraviolet light. Your phone screen: the reason fingerprints wipe off so easily is a coating just a few nanometres thick that grease cannot grip — the same hydrophobic idea from Activity C, pointed at finger-oil. Socks and sports kit: some contain silver nanoparticles, because at that size silver is brilliant at killing the bacteria that make feet smell. Your TV: if it says "QLED", its colours come from quantum dots — crystals a few nanometres across whose colour is set purely by their size (the gold-nanoparticle trick from Part 03, done on purpose). The three scientists who worked out how to make them won the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

The biggest one is invisible. Inside every phone and computer is a chip holding billions of transistors — tiny switches that flick on and off billions of times a second — and the important parts of each switch are only tens of nanometres across. The very first transistor, built in 1947, was the size of your palm. A modern phone chip packs well over 15 billion of them into a square the size of your fingernail. Every video game, every video call and every word on this page exists because engineers learned to build at the nanoscale.

And then there is medicine. You may have heard of "nano-robots" swimming through your blood to fix things. Real, tiny, swimming robots that make decisions are still science fiction — for now; scientists have built simple prototypes in labs, but none is treating patients yet. What is real is almost as good: doctors wrap medicine inside nanoparticles about 100 nm across that carry it through the body and release it in the right place, so a cancer drug reaches the tumour instead of everywhere. The vaccines that ended the COVID pandemic delivered their instructions inside fat-bubble nanoparticles exactly like this — nanotechnology has already been injected into billions of arms. Nano is not the future. It is the present, and it is already in your pocket.

People say a new chip is built with "3-nanometre transistors." That number is a name, not a measurement. The real switches inside a "3 nm" chip are more like 10–20 nm across — still staggeringly small, but the label stopped matching the ruler years ago and now works more like a model year on a car. Also: a nanometre-scale transistor is not a "nano-robot". It cannot move or decide anything on its own. It is a very, very small light switch — and a chip is a few billion of them wired together.
The idea of nanotechnology was first proposed in a talk in 1959 by the physicist Richard Feynman, called "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom". He offered a $1,000 prize to anyone who could shrink a page of a book down 25,000 times — small enough to fit the entire Encyclopædia Britannica on the head of a pin. It took until 1985 for a student to claim it, using an electron beam. Today's chips write far, far smaller than that, billions of times a day.

◆ THE THREE THINGS TO REMEMBER

1. Everything is atoms, and the arrangement is the recipe. Same carbon atoms make a pencil or a diamond. Same sugar dissolves fast or slow depending only on how finely it is cut.
2. Materials behave differently at tiny scales. Shrink something to the nanoscale and its surface takes over: it reacts faster, changes colour, gets stronger, grips harder — gold goes red, zinc oxide goes transparent, a pencil line becomes a wire.
3. Nanotechnology lets us design materials on purpose. Nature did it first with the lotus leaf and the gecko's foot. Now engineers do it in sunscreen, chips, screens, medicine and vaccines — and the next material is waiting for someone to sketch it.
A gecko can hang from a glass ceiling by one toe. Its feet are not sticky and have no suckers — each toe is covered in millions of hairs that split into nano-sized tips, so many of them touching the glass that the tiny pull every atom feels for every other atom adds up to enough to hold the whole lizard. Scientists have copied it into a reusable "gecko tape" — one of the discoverers of graphene worked on it first.

◷ LATEST NUMBERS — CHECKED SEPTEMBER 2026

Nanotechnology moves fast, so the numbers most likely to go out of date live in this box.

Transistors per phone chip: the best phone chips of 2025–26 hold roughly 15–20 billion transistors; the biggest AI chips hold over 200 billion.
Chip "node" names: "2 nm" chips began shipping in 2025–26. Remember the myth-buster in Part 05 — the name is not the size.
Nano-robots in medicine: still laboratory prototypes as of 2026 — none approved for treating patients. Nanoparticle drug carriers, on the other hand, have been in clinics since the 1990s.
Graphene: found in some tennis rackets, running shoes, bike tyres, headphones and phone cooling systems, and in a small number of concrete and battery products. Not yet the "everything material" it was once promised to be.
Quantum dots: the 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry (Bawendi, Brus and Ekimov) — now standard in QLED televisions and monitors.
