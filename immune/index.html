MODULE 01 🛡️ What Is the Immune System? 9 min ▼

Right now, while you read this, you are under attack. Bacteria are landing on your skin, viruses are drifting into your nose, and fungal spores are floating past your eyes. You have no idea it is happening — because you have an army inside you, and it is winning. The immune system is not one organ you could point to. It is a whole network: your skin, your snot, your blood, your bone marrow, your thymus, hundreds of lymph nodes and your spleen, all working as one. Its single most important trick is telling "self" from "non-self" — which cells are you, and which ones are intruders. And it defends you in three layers: walls that keep germs out, a rapid response team that swarms anything that gets in, and a squad of specialists that learns each enemy by name and never forgets it.

Your gut is your immune system’s busiest border. It holds the biggest single patch of immune tissue in your body and makes more antibodies than anywhere else in you — which makes sense, because the inside of your intestines is one of the few places the outside world gets deep inside you. You will often read that “70% of your immune system is in your gut”. That number gets repeated everywhere, but when scientists actually counted the cells in 2023 they found most of your immune cells live in your bone marrow and lymph nodes instead. Even famous facts get corrected — that is science working properly.
MODULE 02 🧱 First Line of Defence: The Castle Walls 10 min ▼

The best fight is the one that never starts. Your first line of defence is a set of walls and traps that stop germs before they are ever inside you — and it works on almost everything, all the time, without needing to know what the germ is. Your skin is the biggest one: about one and a half to two square metres of waterproof, germ-proof armour, and unbroken skin is nearly impossible for bacteria to cross. Where you cannot have skin — eyes, nose, mouth, lungs, gut — you have wet defences instead. Mucus (yes, snot) is sticky flypaper that traps whatever you breathe in, and microscopic hairs called cilia sweep it up your throat like a tiny escalator so you swallow it. Tears and saliva carry an enzyme called lysozyme that splits bacteria open. Your stomach acid drops to about pH 1.5 to 2 when it is working hardest — more sour than lemon juice — and most swallowed germs simply dissolve. Even your good bacteria help: tens of trillions of friendly microbes cover your skin and gut, and they take up all the space and food so dangerous ones cannot move in. Sneezing, coughing, crying and weeing are all part of it too — every one of them physically flushes germs out.

You swallow roughly a litre of your own mucus every single day and never notice. Your airways make it constantly, the cilia escalator sweeps it up to your throat, and down it goes — carrying every trapped dust speck, pollen grain and bacterium straight into your stomach acid, which finishes them off.
MODULE 03 🚨 Second Line of Defence: The Rapid Response Team 12 min ▼

Step on a splinter and the wall is breached. Within seconds your second line of defence kicks in — and this one does not care what kind of germ it is, it just attacks anything that should not be there. This is called innate immunity, the defence you were born with — your barriers in Module 02 were innate too, and this is the innate system’s second wave, the one that fights back once something is already inside. Damaged cells release chemical alarm signals. Blood vessels around the wound widen and get leaky, so the area goes red, hot, swollen and sore — that is inflammation, and it is not the injury, it is the rescue. Through those leaky vessels pour the phagocytes, cells whose whole job is eating. Neutrophils arrive first, in enormous numbers, within minutes; they are the most common white blood cell in your blood, they swallow bacteria whole, and they live only about a day before dying at the scene. Macrophages — Greek for "big eaters" — arrive next: bigger, slower, longer-lived, and they clean up the mess afterwards. And if your temperature climbs, that is deliberate. A fever above 38 °C makes your body a worse place for many germs to breed and makes your own immune cells work faster. Feeling rubbish is often the sound of your army mobilising — though a fever that climbs very high, or lasts more than a day or two, always needs a grown-up to check on you.

MODULE 04 🎯 The Specialists: T Cells, B Cells & Antibodies 14 min ▼

Some germs are too clever for brute force. That is when your third line wakes up — the adaptive immune system, which does not just fight the enemy, it studies it. A macrophage or a dendritic cell that has eaten an invader holds up a piece of it like a wanted poster. That piece is called an antigen. Helper T cells read the poster and become the commanders: they raise the alarm and switch on everyone else. Killer T cells hunt down your own cells that a virus has taken over and destroy them — sad, but necessary, because a hijacked cell is a virus factory. B cells take a different approach: each one builds antibodies, Y-shaped proteins that lock onto one exact antigen shape and nothing else, like a key cut for a single lock. Antibodies clump germs together, glue them still, and mark them so phagocytes know exactly what to eat. Between them, your B cells can build more than a billion different antibody shapes — enough to match germs you have never even met. And when the fight is over, some cells stay behind forever as memory cells. That is why you almost never catch chickenpox twice. Next time, the response that took a week takes hours.

T cells get their letter from the thymus, a small organ behind your breastbone where they go to train — and almost any T cell that would attack your own body gets destroyed there before it can graduate. B cells got their letter from a completely different place: an organ in birds called the bursa of Fabricius, where scientists first found them. In you, B cells are made in your bone marrow — which is a lucky coincidence for anyone trying to remember it.
MODULE 05 ⚔️ Germs, Vaccines, Allergies & Staying Healthy 15 min ▼

Not all germs are the same, and your body does not fight them the same way. Bacteria are complete living cells — they eat, they grow, they split in two, and they can multiply happily in your body all by themselves. Phagocytes eat them and antibodies clump them. Viruses are not really alive at all: a virus is basically a set of instructions in a shell, and it can do nothing until it breaks into one of your cells and forces it to build copies. That is why killer T cells have to destroy your own infected cells to stop it. It is also why antibiotics do not work on a cold — antibiotics attack machinery that only bacteria have, and a virus does not have any of it. Taking them anyway does real damage. In any big group of bacteria a few are already resistant purely by luck — so wiping out all the others just leaves the field clear for the tough ones to take over. That is not the bacteria learning; that is the antibiotic doing the choosing. And those survivors are much harder to treat next time.

Which brings us to the cleverest medical trick ever invented. A vaccine shows your immune system a harmless preview of a germ — a dead one, a weakened one, one piece of its outer coat, or a set of instructions that teaches your own cells to build that piece, which is how mRNA vaccines work — so your B cells and T cells can train against it and leave memory cells behind. You get the immunity without ever getting the disease. In 1796 a doctor called Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who caught mild cowpox never got deadly smallpox, and tested the idea on an eight-year-old boy named James Phipps. The word "vaccine" comes from vacca, Latin for cow. In 1980 the World Health Organization declared smallpox eradicated — wiped off the planet, the only human disease we have ever managed it with. And when enough people around you are immune, a germ runs out of people to jump to and dies out before it reaches the few who cannot be vaccinated. That is herd immunity, and you can play with it below.

Sometimes the system gets it wrong. An allergy is your immune system attacking something completely harmless — pollen, peanuts, cat fur — as though it were a deadly invader. It makes a type of antibody called IgE, which triggers cells to dump histamine, and histamine is what makes you sneeze, itch, stream and swell. That is why one common kind of allergy medicine is called an antihistamine. A few allergies can turn serious very fast, and antihistamines are nowhere near strong enough for that — people at risk carry an adrenaline pen instead and have a plan they have practised. The rule for everyone else is simple: if someone is reacting badly, tell an adult immediately. Looking after the whole system is refreshingly ordinary: sleep, real food, exercise, and washing your hands for twenty seconds. In one famous study, volunteers were deliberately given a cold virus — and the ones who had been sleeping under six hours a night were about four times more likely to actually come down with the cold than the ones getting seven or more. Your army fights better when it is rested.
